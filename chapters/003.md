## 3. From One Came All

> **And as all things came from One, through the mediation of One, so all things were born from this One Thing by adaptation.**

### A Note on the Translation

The third saying is among the most difficult in the *Emerald Tablet* because several of its key words have been translated in different ways. Some versions speak of "meditation," others of "mediation," "adaptation," "adoption," or "transformation." The differences arise because the surviving Arabic and Latin manuscripts do not always preserve identical wording, and some of the terms carry several possible meanings.

Despite these variations, the central idea remains remarkably consistent. The world is not portrayed as a collection of unrelated objects. Rather, all things originate from a single source and continue to participate in a deeper unity. Diversity is real, but beneath diversity lies an underlying principle from which everything proceeds.

For this commentary, the translation above captures both the historical ambiguity and the philosophical thrust of the passage. The emphasis falls not upon the mechanics of creation but upon the conviction that multiplicity arises from unity. The many are expressions of the One without ceasing to be genuinely many.

### Historical Background

This saying reflects one of the defining characteristics of Hermetic philosophy: reality is fundamentally unified. The visible diversity of the cosmos is not ultimate. Behind the countless forms of nature lies a single originating principle from which all things emerge and to which, in some sense, they remain connected.

This conviction was not unique to Hermeticism. Similar themes appear throughout the philosophical world of late antiquity. Plato had spoken of eternal Forms behind the changing world. The Stoics described the universe as permeated by the divine Logos. Later, Neoplatonists such as Plotinus would describe all existence as flowing from "the One," the inexhaustible source of all being.

The *Emerald Tablet* expresses this intuition in remarkably concise language. Rather than offering a detailed cosmology, it simply assumes that unity precedes diversity. The world is not a random assortment of disconnected parts but the unfolding of a deeper order.

Whether the author imagined this process as literal emanation, symbolic generation, or something else entirely remains uncertain. The brevity of the Tablet invites reflection more than explanation, which may partly account for its enduring fascination.

### Some Common Interpretations

#### 1. The Neoplatonic Interpretation

Many readers understand this saying through the lens of Neoplatonism. According to this tradition, all reality flows from the One as light radiates from the sun. The One does not diminish by giving rise to the many, nor does creation stand outside its sustaining source. Everything participates in the goodness of its origin while remaining distinct from it.

This interpretation became enormously influential in late antiquity and shaped much of medieval philosophy. The language of "emanation" should not be understood as a moment in time but as an ongoing dependence. Everything exists because it continually receives being from the One.

#### 2. The Alchemical Interpretation

For alchemists, the "One Thing" often referred to the mysterious primal substance underlying all material reality. If every metal ultimately shares the same origin, then transformation from one form into another becomes conceivable. Gold and lead would not be utterly unrelated substances but different expressions of the same underlying reality.

Under this reading, the saying establishes the theoretical basis for alchemy itself. Transformation becomes possible because diversity conceals an underlying unity. The work of the alchemist is not to create something entirely new but to reveal what has been hidden within matter from the beginning.

#### 3. A Cosmological Interpretation

Others understand the saying more broadly as a statement about the coherence of creation. Every part of the universe belongs within a single ordered whole. Mountains, rivers, stars, animals, and human beings are not isolated realities but participants in one living cosmos.

This interpretation encourages a profound reverence for nature. To study any part of creation is, in some measure, to glimpse the wisdom expressed throughout the whole. Knowledge becomes an act of discovering relationships rather than merely collecting facts.

#### 4. A Psychological Interpretation

Modern readers often internalize the saying. Instead of viewing the "One" as a cosmic principle alone, they understand it as the hidden center of the human person. Thoughts, emotions, memories, desires, and aspirations may appear fragmented, yet beneath them lies a deeper self seeking integration.

From this perspective, spiritual growth is the gradual recovery of interior unity. The divided self slowly becomes whole as every aspect of life is brought into relationship with its true center. The alchemical work becomes the healing of the human soul.

#### 5. A Perennial Philosophy Interpretation

Some writers have seen this saying as evidence that all religions ultimately describe the same reality. Because all things come from One, every spiritual tradition is viewed as another expression of the same universal wisdom.

While this interpretation encourages dialogue and mutual respect, it can also flatten important differences. Similar questions do not always lead to identical answers. Hermeticism, Platonism, Buddhism, Judaism, Christianity, and Islam each understand the source of reality in distinctive ways, even when they share the intuition that existence possesses a profound unity.

### A Christian Mystical Reading

Few themes resonate more deeply with Christian contemplation than the conviction that creation possesses an underlying unity. The opening verses of John's Gospel proclaim that all things came into being through the eternal Word, and apart from that Word nothing came into existence. Likewise, Paul declares that "all things hold together" in Christ. Christianity therefore agrees that the many arise from one source, but it identifies that source not as an abstract principle but as the living God.

This distinction matters. The Christian faith does not begin with an impersonal unity from which persons eventually emerge. It begins with the God who eternally exists as communion: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Unity is therefore not solitary. At the heart of reality is relationship, love, and self-giving fellowship. Creation reflects this communion because it springs from divine generosity rather than necessity.

Seen through this lens, the diversity of creation is not a problem to be overcome but a gift to be celebrated. A forest is beautiful precisely because it contains countless different forms of life held together in a living ecosystem. Likewise, the Apostle Paul describes the Church as one body composed of many members. Unity does not erase distinction; it gives distinction its proper harmony.

The Incarnation deepens this vision even further. In Christ, God does not merely sustain creation from beyond it but enters creation from within. The eternal Word assumes human nature without ceasing to be divine. The One from whom all things came joins the many so that the many might be gathered into communion without losing their uniqueness.

Christian mysticism therefore understands salvation not as the dissolution of individuality but as its fulfillment. We do not disappear into God like drops returning to an ocean. Rather, we become fully ourselves as divine love heals the fragmentation that obscures our true identity. The saints are not interchangeable because God's unity is spacious enough to embrace infinite personal uniqueness.

This perspective also reshapes the way we understand our neighbors. If every person ultimately derives life from the same Creator, then every encounter becomes holy. The stranger, the enemy, and the friend alike bear the imprint of the One who gives existence to all. Love of neighbor is therefore not merely an ethical command but a recognition of reality. To wound another is, in some measure, to deny the unity that God has woven into creation.

The Christian tradition has often spoken of Christ as the one in whom "all things are summed up." This does not mean that every difference disappears. Rather, the fractured pieces of creation are gradually reconciled through divine love. The story of redemption is the story of scattered humanity being drawn toward communion without coercion. Love creates unity while preserving freedom.

### Contemplative Synthesis

The third saying invites us to look beyond appearances. The world often seems fragmented, divided into competing interests, isolated individuals, and conflicting identities. Yet beneath that fragmentation lies a deeper unity that continually calls creation toward harmony.

Hermetic philosophy saw this unity in the hidden structure of the cosmos. Christian faith recognizes that same longing but discovers its fulfillment in the triune God revealed in Christ. The One is not merely the beginning of creation but its sustaining life and its ultimate destination.

When we learn to see every person, every creature, and every moment as held within that divine love, we begin to inhabit the world differently. Gratitude replaces possession, compassion overcomes rivalry, and contemplation becomes the quiet recognition that everything lives, moves, and has its being in God.

### Questions for Reflection

Where do I experience fragmentation within my own life?

How might seeing every person as sharing one divine source change the way I treat them?

Do I understand unity as uniformity, or as love holding together genuine diversity?

In what ways is Christ inviting the scattered pieces of my life into deeper communion?

### Prayer

Eternal God,
from You all things come,
through You all things are sustained,
and to You all things return.

Gather together what has become divided within me.
Teach me to delight in the beautiful diversity of Your creation without losing sight of the love that binds it together.

May I recognize Christ as the One in whom all things hold together, and may my life become an instrument of reconciliation wherever I go.

Amen.
