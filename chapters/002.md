## 2. As Above, So Below

> **What is above is like what is below, and what is below is like what is above, to accomplish the wonders of the One Thing.**

### A Note on the Translation

The familiar phrase **“As above, so below”** is a shortened paraphrase of the second saying of the *Emerald Tablet*. A common Latin version reads:

> *Quod est superius est sicut quod inferius, et quod inferius est sicut quod est superius, ad perpetranda miracula rei unius.*

Isaac Newton translated it:

> “That which is below is like that which is above, and that which is above is like that which is below, to do the miracles of one only thing.”

The compressed modern phrase has become so famous that it is sometimes treated as though it were the entire saying. But the final clause is essential:

> **“…to accomplish the wonders of the One Thing.”**

The saying is not merely claiming that heaven and earth resemble one another. It suggests that their correspondence makes possible a work of transformation. The above and the below participate in a single reality, and recognizing—or perhaps joining—them allows the “wonder” of the One Thing to be accomplished.

For readability, we will use:

> **What is above is like what is below, and what is below is like what is above, to accomplish the wonders of the One Thing.**

The words **“like”** and **“correspond”** are important. The saying does not necessarily claim that above and below are identical. Rather, one reflects, resembles, or reveals something about the other.

The phrase “as above, so below” is therefore useful, but it can oversimplify the richer statement from which it comes. The surviving versions place the correspondence within the context of a single transformative work.

### Historical Background

The second saying is the best-known passage in the *Emerald Tablet* and perhaps the most famous summary of Hermetic thought. It expresses a relationship between different levels of reality: heaven and earth, invisible and visible, spiritual and material, greater world and smaller world.

This idea is often described through the terms **macrocosm** and **microcosm**.

The macrocosm is the great order—the universe as a whole.

The microcosm is the smaller order—often the human being.

In many ancient and medieval philosophies, humanity was regarded as a small universe containing, in miniature, the same patterns found within the larger cosmos. Human beings and the world were not isolated objects. They belonged to one harmonious order and could therefore illuminate one another.

This does not mean that the saying was originally intended as a modern slogan about positive thinking, personal manifestation, or the power of the mind to command the universe. Those are much later applications. Within the Tablet itself, the saying introduces an alchemical and cosmological mystery: the transformation accomplished when higher and lower realities are brought into correspondence.

The ancient and medieval imagination did not divide reality into the watertight compartments familiar to modern thought. Astronomy, medicine, philosophy, religion, metallurgy, and psychology could all be understood as aspects of one interconnected cosmos.

A bodily process might reflect a celestial process.

The transformation of a metal might reveal a spiritual principle.

The human soul might mirror the order of the heavens.

An alchemist could therefore believe that working with matter was also a way of participating in the deeper patterns of creation.

### Some Common Interpretations

#### 1. Heaven and Earth Correspond

The simplest interpretation is cosmological. The higher world and the lower world follow corresponding patterns.

The movements of the heavens were believed to influence earthly seasons, tides, vegetation, bodies, and temperaments. The celestial realm was not remote from earthly existence. What occurred above was reflected below.

This correspondence did not necessarily mean that every earthly event was mechanically caused by the stars. It meant that the cosmos was an ordered whole whose different levels shared a common structure.

The universe was less like a collection of unrelated objects and more like a living organism.

From this perspective, the saying means:

**The same wisdom that orders the heavens is present within the earth.**

To understand one level of reality may therefore offer insight into another.

#### 2. The Human Being as a Little Cosmos

The saying has frequently been interpreted through the relationship between the macrocosm and the microcosm. The human person is a small world reflecting the larger world.

The body contains earthly matter.

Breath resembles wind.

Bodily warmth resembles fire.

The mind reaches toward the divine or intelligible realm.

Humanity stands at the meeting point of the physical and spiritual worlds.

This interpretation gives the human being a unique dignity and responsibility. We are not merely one object among others. We are a point of convergence—a creature in whom various levels of reality meet.

Knowledge of the self can therefore become knowledge of the cosmos, while contemplation of the cosmos can reveal something about the self.

The great world is reflected in the small.

#### 3. An Alchemical Operation

Within alchemy, “above” and “below” can refer to different states of matter or stages of the alchemical process.

A substance may be heated so that its volatile or subtle elements rise. These may later cool, condense, and descend. What rises and what falls are separated, purified, and eventually reunited.

The saying would then describe more than an abstract philosophy. It would point toward an operation performed within the vessel.

The lower is raised.

The higher descends.

The two exchange their powers.

Through this circulation, transformation occurs.

This helps explain why the sentence concludes with the “wonders of the One Thing.” The correspondence of above and below is not merely observed; it is enacted within the alchemical work.

The laboratory becomes a little cosmos, and the alchemist seeks to reproduce within the vessel the creative patterns of the universe.

#### 4. Spirit and Matter Belong Together

Some readers understand the saying as a rejection of the idea that spirit is good while matter is worthless or evil.

The invisible and visible worlds belong to one reality. Matter can bear spiritual meaning, and spirit can express itself materially. The lower is not abandoned in the ascent toward the higher.

This interpretation became especially fruitful in forms of Renaissance Hermeticism, where nature was often regarded as filled with divine signs, sympathies, and correspondences. The world could be read as a book whose visible forms disclosed invisible wisdom.

Under this interpretation, the saying means:

**The spiritual is expressed through the material, and the material participates in the spiritual.**

Nature is not empty.

It is symbolic.

#### 5. Inner and Outer Life

Modern interpreters commonly extend the saying into the phrase:

> “As within, so without.”

Those exact words are not part of the historical *Emerald Tablet*, but they express a later psychological interpretation of its principle.

The outer life is thought to reflect the inner life. Fear, conflict, desire, and disorder within the person may shape relationships and actions outside. Conversely, outer circumstances may reveal patterns hidden within the psyche.

The saying then becomes a call to interior transformation. We cannot create peace around us while continually feeding hatred within us. We cannot build truthful relationships while remaining committed to self-deception. We cannot heal the world without allowing some healing to occur in ourselves.

Yet this interpretation must be handled carefully. It would be cruel to suggest that every misfortune in a person’s outer life is the manifestation of an inner failure. Poverty, sickness, abuse, oppression, and tragedy cannot simply be blamed upon the thoughts or spiritual condition of those who suffer.

The correspondence between inner and outer life is real, but it is not a formula for blaming victims or imagining that the individual mind controls every event.

#### 6. Psychological Integration

In psychological readings of alchemy, above and below may symbolize different dimensions of the human psyche.

“Above” can represent conscious thought, reason, spirit, ideals, or the person we believe ourselves to be.

“Below” can represent instinct, the body, emotion, the unconscious, rejected memories, and the shadow.

Wholeness is not achieved by allowing the higher to destroy the lower. It comes through recognizing, purifying, and reconciling them.

The spiritual person cannot become whole by despising the body.

The rational person cannot become whole by denying emotion.

The outwardly virtuous person cannot become whole by refusing to face the anger, fear, envy, or woundedness concealed within.

Under this reading, the “wonder of the One Thing” is the birth of an integrated self.

What has been divided is brought into relationship.

#### 7. Magical Correspondence

In some occult traditions, the saying supports the belief that objects, planets, plants, colors, metals, words, and human faculties correspond to one another. An action performed symbolically within the smaller world is believed to affect the greater world.

This is sometimes called sympathetic magic. Because things share hidden correspondences, one may act upon one thing in order to influence another.

This interpretation is historically important, but a Christian reading cannot simply adopt it without discernment. Christianity recognizes symbolic and sacramental relationships, but it does not therefore teach that the divine can be controlled through secret techniques.

There is a profound difference between participating receptively in grace and attempting to manipulate hidden powers.

#### 8. A Principle of Personal Manifestation

In modern popular spirituality, “as above, so below” is sometimes taken to mean that thoughts create external reality. If the mind holds the proper image, the universe will reproduce that image in material circumstances.

This interpretation may encourage people to examine their attitudes and intentions, but it is far removed from the historical setting of the Tablet. It can also reduce spirituality to a method for obtaining wealth, success, health, or control.

The ancient saying is concerned with cosmic correspondence and transformation. It is not a guarantee that desire, visualization, or confidence will make the world obey the individual will.

The “One Thing” is larger than the ego.

### A Christian Mystical Reading

Christianity also speaks of heaven and earth as distinct yet deeply related.

Jesus teaches his disciples to pray:

> “Your kingdom come.
> Your will be done,
> on earth as it is in heaven.”

This prayer might be called a Christian form of “as above, so below”—but with an essential difference. Earth does not automatically reflect heaven. We pray that it may come to do so.

The world as we presently experience it does not perfectly mirror the life of God. Violence, domination, greed, fear, and death distort the divine intention. The Christian does not merely observe a correspondence between heaven and earth; the Christian longs and works for their reconciliation.

What is above must become visible below.

God’s justice must take flesh in human justice.

God’s mercy must become earthly mercy.

God’s forgiveness must enter wounded relationships.

God’s peace must become the peacemaking of communities.

The prayer of Jesus is that the hidden life of God might appear within the visible world.

### The Incarnation: Heaven and Earth Meet

For Christian faith, the deepest union of above and below is not an abstract cosmic law but the Incarnation.

> “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us.”

The eternal Word enters time.

The invisible God becomes visible.

The Creator takes the life of a creature.

Heaven does not remain safely above while humanity struggles below. In Christ, God descends into the depths of embodied life—birth, hunger, friendship, sorrow, suffering, and death.

Yet the Incarnation is not merely the descent of heaven. It is also the lifting of humanity into communion with God.

Christ descends so that humanity may ascend.

God becomes human so that human beings may participate in the divine life.

The early Christian tradition sometimes expressed this mystery with daring language: the Son of God became what we are so that we might become, by grace, what he is by nature. This does not mean that human beings become independent gods or cease to be creatures. It means that we are drawn into God’s own life of love.

In Christ, above and below meet without confusion and without separation.

Divinity does not consume humanity.

Humanity does not diminish divinity.

The two are united in love.

### Christ as the One Thing

The *Emerald Tablet* speaks of accomplishing “the wonders of the One Thing.”

A Christian contemplative may hear in this phrase an echo—not an original prediction, but an echo—of the cosmic Christ described in the New Testament:

> “All things have been created through him and for him. He himself is before all things, and in him all things hold together.”

Christ is not one spiritual being within the universe. He is the divine Word through whom the universe exists and in whom it finds its unity.

All things come from God through the Word.

All things are sustained through the Word.

All things are being reconciled through the Word.

From this perspective, the One Thing is not a magical substance or hidden technique. It is the mystery of divine love holding creation together.

The wonder is reconciliation.

God and humanity are reconciled.

Human beings are reconciled to one another.

Spirit and body are restored to harmony.

Heaven and earth are gathered into Christ.

The fragmented creation is drawn toward unity without losing its beautiful diversity.

### Humanity as Microcosm

Christian thinkers have also described the human being as a microcosm. We belong to the material creation through our bodies, yet we are capable of consciousness, freedom, love, prayer, and communion with God.

In us, dust learns to speak.

Matter becomes capable of praise.

The earth lifts its voice toward heaven.

Gregory of Nyssa sees human beings as standing at a mysterious meeting point within creation. We share bodily life with animals and spiritual capacities with the invisible realm. Our vocation is not to reject one side for the other, but to gather our whole being into love of God.

The spiritual path is therefore not an escape from embodiment.

We pray with bodies.

We love through bodies.

We suffer in bodies.

We receive bread and wine.

We wash one another’s feet.

We touch the sick.

We bury the dead.

Christian holiness does not leave the lower world behind. It allows divine life to become visible within it.

Grace does not destroy nature.

Grace heals and fulfills it.

### The Sacramental Vision

The second saying can also be placed in conversation with the Christian sacramental imagination.

A sacrament is an outward and visible reality through which invisible grace is communicated. Water becomes the sign and bearer of new birth. Bread and wine become communion. Oil becomes healing and consecration. Human touch becomes blessing.

This does not mean that the material object is merely a code pointing elsewhere. Nor does it mean that the object possesses magical power which human beings can control.

Rather, the visible creation is capable of bearing divine presence because it was created through the Word and is sustained within God.

The world is not God.

But neither is it godless.

Creation can become transparent to grace.

A flower, a meal, a human face, the night sky, a moment of silence—all may become places where the heart awakens to the divine presence. The contemplative does not worship the created thing but receives it as gift and sign.

The lower reveals the higher because the higher has lovingly given existence to the lower.

### The Pattern Revealed in Christ

Christian mysticism also finds correspondence between the life of Christ and the life of the disciple.

Christ’s pattern is reproduced within us:

He descends before he ascends.

He loses his life and receives it again.

He empties himself and is exalted.

He enters death and brings forth resurrection.

The Christian path follows the same shape—not because we repeat Christ’s saving work, but because his life becomes the form of ours.

What is above becomes what is below.

The life of Christ is formed within the disciple.

His humility becomes our humility.

His compassion becomes our compassion.

His forgiveness becomes our forgiveness.

His death to self becomes our liberation from the false self.

His resurrection becomes the beginning of new life within us.

Christian transformation is not the imitation of Christ from a distance. It is participation in Christ through the Spirit.

The pattern above is written below.

### Within and Without

Although “as within, so without” is not part of the Tablet itself, it can become a fruitful Christian meditation.

Jesus repeatedly directs attention toward the heart. He warns against cleaning the outside while leaving the inside full of greed and self-indulgence. He teaches that words and actions arise from what has been cultivated within.

Christian transformation must therefore move inward.

But it cannot remain there.

Contemplation that does not become compassion is incomplete. Prayer that never changes how we treat people has not yet reached its fulfillment. Inner peace that depends upon ignoring the suffering of others is not the peace of Christ.

The movement must go both ways.

The outer life reveals the inner life.

The inner life gradually reshapes the outer life.

We receive mercy inwardly and become merciful outwardly.

We encounter forgiveness and begin forgiving.

We discover that we are beloved and become capable of loving without possession.

The hidden kingdom takes visible form.

### An Important Christian Caution

The saying can become spiritually dangerous when it is treated as a formula of control.

Christian prayer is not an attempt to compel the higher world to produce the outcome we desire below. Sacraments are not magical techniques. Faith is not a means of mastering God, nature, or other people.

Jesus reveals divine power through self-emptying love, not domination.

God’s relationship with creation is not mechanical. It is personal, free, and loving. We do not learn the correct correspondence and thereby force heaven to obey us.

We surrender.

We listen.

We participate.

We consent to grace.

The Christian mystic does not seek secret knowledge in order to rise above ordinary humanity. The mystic descends with Christ into greater solidarity with it.

Any spirituality that makes us feel superior to the uninitiated has misunderstood the ascent.

The way upward is downward.

The way to God passes through love of neighbor.

The heavenly is proven by the earthly fruit it bears.

### Contemplative Synthesis

The second saying proclaims that reality is interconnected. The higher and lower worlds are not sealed off from one another. They correspond, reflect, and participate in a greater unity.

The alchemist saw this unity within the transforming vessel.

The philosopher saw it in the relationship between cosmos and humanity.

The psychologist sees it in the dialogue between consciousness and the hidden depths of the self.

The Christian sees its fullest revelation in Christ.

In him, heaven descends to earth.

In him, humanity is lifted into God.

In him, matter becomes the dwelling place of divine glory.

In him, the scattered fragments of creation are gathered into communion.

“As above, so below” therefore becomes more than a theory about cosmic resemblance. It becomes a vocation.

Let the love that eternally flows within God flow through us.

Let heavenly mercy appear within earthly relationships.

Let divine communion become human community.

Let what we contemplate in prayer take flesh in the way we live.

The question is not merely whether the world below resembles the world above.

The question is whether our lives do.

### Questions for Reflection

Where do I separate the spiritual from the ordinary?

Does my prayer become visible in the way I treat others?

What part of my embodied or emotional life have I wrongly regarded as unworthy of God?

Where might Christ be seeking to unite what I have divided?

What would it mean for God’s will to be done through me “on earth as it is in heaven”?

Does my spirituality lead me toward greater control, or toward deeper surrender and love?

### Prayer

God of heaven and earth,
in Christ You have joined
what I had imagined was divided.

You have entered matter,
time, suffering, and death,
filling human life with Your presence.

Bring together what is divided within me.

Let my body and spirit,
my prayer and action,
my inner life and outward relationships
become one offering of love.

May the mercy I contemplate above
take form in the world below.

May Your kingdom come in me,
Your will be done through me,
and Your love become visible
in every ordinary place.

Gather all things into Christ,
in whom heaven and earth
are reconciled.

Amen.
