## 6. The Source of Every Wonder

> **It is the source of every work of wonder in the world. Its power is complete.**

### A Note on the Translation

This saying contains one of the most difficult words in the *Emerald Tablet*. The term often appears in Latin forms as *telesmi* or *telesma*, words whose meanings can include completion, perfection, consecrated work, talisman, wonder, or something brought successfully to its intended end. This explains why translations differ so widely, offering phrases such as “the father of all perfection,” “the father of every magical work,” “the source of all wonders,” or “the father of all things accomplished in the world.”

The version supplied here reads, “It is the father of all works of wonder in the world.” That translation preserves the generative imagery of the preceding saying, in which the mysterious One Thing is conceived by the Sun and Moon, carried by the wind, and nourished by the earth. Yet the English phrase “father of all works of wonder” can sound awkward, as though the text were suddenly assigning another masculine role to the substance it has just described as a child.

For clarity, we will use the translation, **“It is the source of every work of wonder in the world. Its power is complete.”** The word “source” preserves the idea of origin without becoming entangled in the changing parental imagery. “Work of wonder” retains the breadth of *telesma*, suggesting both an extraordinary accomplishment and a work brought to completion.

The second sentence, “Its power is complete,” is equally important. The Latin word *integra* can mean whole, entire, intact, or undiminished. The saying therefore describes a power that is not partial, fragmented, or deficient. Whatever the One Thing may be, it contains within itself everything necessary to accomplish its work.

### Historical Background

This saying marks a transition within the *Emerald Tablet*. The preceding lines describe the origin of the One Thing through the cooperation of Sun, Moon, wind, and earth. The text now turns from its formation to its effectiveness, declaring that this mysterious reality is the source of every marvelous or perfected work in the world.

Within the alchemical tradition, the passage was often associated with the philosopher’s stone. The stone was believed to possess a universal power capable of perfecting imperfect metals, healing bodily corruption, or accelerating processes that nature itself performed only slowly. Its power was “complete” because it contained the harmonized forces from which material transformation proceeded.

The language of *telesma* also carried religious and magical associations. In some contexts, it referred to an object fashioned or consecrated to receive celestial influence, giving rise to the later word “talisman.” A *telesma* could therefore be more than an impressive deed; it might be a completed vessel through which a hidden power became effective in the visible world.

Yet the root idea of the word is completion. A work of wonder is not necessarily something that violates nature. It may be the bringing of a hidden potential to fulfillment. The alchemist’s wonder consisted in cooperating with the inner tendencies of matter until what was incomplete reached its proper perfection.

The saying therefore joins power with wholeness. The One Thing is effective because it is undivided. It can bring other things toward completion because its own power has first been made complete.

### Some Common Interpretations

#### 1. The Philosopher’s Stone as Universal Agent

The most direct alchemical interpretation identifies the One Thing with the philosopher’s stone. This was not usually imagined as an ordinary stone but as a perfected substance capable of transforming other substances. It represented the successful completion of the alchemical work and the agent through which further transformations could be accomplished.

According to this reading, the stone is the “source of every work of wonder” because it contains the reconciled powers of Sun and Moon, sulfur and mercury, volatility and stability. The stone is no longer internally divided. Its opposing qualities have been purified and joined into a harmonious whole.

Its power is complete because nothing essential is lacking. It does not need some additional principle from outside itself to finish the work. Having reached perfection, it can communicate perfection to what remains incomplete.

The alchemist’s goal was therefore not simply to discover a powerful object. It was to bring matter into such profound harmony that its hidden possibilities became active. Power arose from integration rather than force.

#### 2. The Perfection of Metals

Medieval alchemists often believed that metals developed within the earth and tended naturally toward perfection. Gold was considered the most complete metal, while other metals represented incomplete or corrupted stages within the same basic process. The alchemist sought to assist nature by removing whatever prevented a metal from reaching its intended form.

Under this interpretation, the “works of wonder” are acts of material perfection. Lead or another base metal is not turned arbitrarily into something unrelated to itself. Rather, its concealed golden potential is awakened and brought to maturity.

The saying reflects a world in which nature is purposeful. Matter is not dead substance lacking direction; it contains tendencies, possibilities, and a movement toward fulfillment. The alchemist’s art succeeds when it cooperates with that movement.

Although modern chemistry does not accept this medieval theory of metallic development, the philosophical intuition remains evocative. Transformation is not always the imposition of a foreign identity. It may be the release of a possibility already hidden within a thing.

#### 3. A Talismanic or Magical Interpretation

Because *telesma* can refer to a consecrated or magically effective object, some interpreters understand the saying as referring to talismanic power. A talisman was believed to embody celestial influences within material form. By preparing an object at the proper time and according to the correct correspondences, the practitioner sought to make the powers of the greater cosmos active within the smaller world.

The One Thing would then be the fundamental source from which every effective talisman or magical operation receives power. Its completeness allows it to mediate between higher and lower realms. What is invisible becomes concentrated within a visible form.

This interpretation belongs to the historical reception of Hermetic and alchemical traditions, but it also reveals their recurring desire to gain access to hidden power. Knowledge of correspondences could easily become a means of attempting to influence events, health, fortune, or other people.

A Christian mystical approach must distinguish carefully between sacramental participation and magical control. Christianity affirms that material things can bear grace, but it does not therefore promise that properly arranged symbols will compel divine power to obey human intention.

#### 4. The One Thing as the Universal Principle of Nature

Some philosophical readers understand the saying as a description of the single creative principle operating throughout nature. The many remarkable processes of the world—growth, generation, healing, transformation, and renewal—are expressions of one underlying power. The One Thing is not necessarily an object but the unity from which all natural activity proceeds.

Its power is complete because it operates everywhere without being divided into unrelated forces. The same life that unfolds in a seed appears differently in an animal, a human body, a star, or a changing season. Diversity conceals a deeper coherence.

Under this interpretation, the world itself is the work of wonder. The birth of a child, the formation of a crystal, the healing of a wound, and the growth of a forest all reveal a power moving from hidden potential toward visible form. Wonder is not confined to the supernatural but is present within existence itself.

The saying encourages a contemplative attention to ordinary processes. What becomes familiar can cease to astonish us, yet familiarity does not make it less mysterious. The natural world remains filled with accomplishments no human intelligence could have independently designed or sustained.

#### 5. Spiritual Transformation as the Great Work

Esoteric and spiritual interpreters often read the alchemical work as a transformation of the human person. The “works of wonder” then include the purification of desire, the awakening of consciousness, the reconciliation of opposites, and the formation of an integrated self. The One Thing represents the hidden center from which this transformation becomes possible.

Its power is complete because wholeness already lies as a possibility within the depths of the person. The spiritual path does not construct an entirely new being from nothing. It uncovers, purifies, and brings to maturity the life that has been obscured by fear, ignorance, and fragmentation.

The wonder is therefore not the acquisition of supernatural abilities. It is the emergence of a human being who is no longer ruled by divided motives. Thought, desire, body, imagination, and action begin to move toward a common center.

In this reading, the Great Work is complete when the person becomes internally coherent. The outer life gradually reflects the deepest truth of the soul, and the person becomes capable of acting from wholeness rather than compulsion.

#### 6. Psychological Wholeness as Power

A psychological reading understands completeness as integration. Much of human energy is consumed by inner conflict: one part of the self wants intimacy while another fears it; one part seeks change while another clings to familiarity; one part presents goodness while another hides anger, envy, or grief. Fragmentation weakens the person because the psyche continually works against itself.

When these divided aspects are acknowledged and brought into relationship, energy becomes available for creative life. The person no longer needs to devote the same strength to denial, repression, performance, or self-protection. Wholeness becomes a form of power.

This power is not domination over others. It is the capacity to remain present, act truthfully, and endure complexity without collapsing into old defenses. A whole person may become less impressive outwardly while becoming far more grounded inwardly.

The saying can therefore be heard as a claim that genuine effectiveness flows from integration. What is internally divided may still produce activity, but it cannot produce lasting peace. Complete power emerges from a center that no longer depends upon inner violence.

#### 7. Human Creativity and Accomplishment

The word *telesma* may also suggest anything successfully completed. The saying can therefore be read as a meditation upon creativity itself. Every finished work begins as an unrealized possibility and passes through conception, gestation, labor, and embodiment.

This interpretation connects naturally with the preceding saying. The Sun provides the initiating spark, the Moon receives it, the wind carries it, and the earth nourishes it. Only after these movements does the work reach completion and become capable of affecting the world.

Human creativity follows a similar pattern. An idea alone is not yet a work. It must be welcomed, carried through uncertainty, given disciplined form, and released into embodied existence.

The power of a completed work lies in its wholeness. A finished poem, invention, act of reconciliation, or life of faithful service possesses an influence that an unrealized intention does not. Completion makes hidden possibility communicable.

#### 8. The Danger of Fantasies of Omnipotence

The saying’s language of complete power can easily be misread as a promise of unlimited personal control. Some esoteric traditions have imagined the perfected adept as one who masters nature, commands invisible forces, or escapes ordinary human limitations. The spiritual path then becomes a quest for invulnerability.

This temptation is not confined to occult traditions. Religious people may also seek certainty, authority, miraculous signs, or divine favor as protections against vulnerability. Spiritual power becomes attractive because it seems to offer an escape from dependence, weakness, and mortality.

Yet the claim that a principle possesses complete power does not necessarily mean that an individual can seize that power for the ego. The One Thing, within the logic of the Tablet, belongs to the unity of the cosmos rather than to private ambition. To reduce it to personal advantage would be to fragment what the saying presents as whole.

Any responsible spiritual interpretation must therefore ask what kind of power is being described. Is it the power to dominate, or the power to bring things toward completion? Is it the power to compel, or the power to heal, reconcile, and make whole?

### A Christian Mystical Reading

Christianity also speaks of a power through which creation comes into being and reaches fulfillment. Yet this power is not understood primarily as an impersonal force or universal substance. It is the power of the living God, revealed most fully in Jesus Christ.

The New Testament describes Christ as the one through whom all things were created, in whom all things hold together, and through whom God is reconciling all things. A Christian contemplative may therefore hear in the Tablet’s language an echo of the conviction that every true work of healing, beauty, restoration, and transformation ultimately arises from one divine source.

This does not mean that the One Thing of the Tablet was historically intended to signify Christ. The original text belongs to an alchemical world with its own symbols and purposes. Yet Christian contemplation may receive its imagery and ask how the longing for a complete and universal power is answered by the Gospel.

The answer Christianity gives is surprising. The complete power of God is not revealed most clearly through domination, invulnerability, or spectacle. It is revealed in the self-giving love of Christ.

### Christ, the Source of the New Creation

The Gospel of John presents the eternal Word as the source of all created life. Everything comes into being through him, and his life is the light of humanity. Creation is therefore already Christ-shaped before the Incarnation occurs within history.

In Jesus, this creative Word enters the world he sustains. His works of healing, liberation, forgiveness, and restoration are signs of creation being returned to its intended wholeness. The blind see, the excluded are welcomed, the tormented find peace, and sinners recover their dignity.

These acts are not displays of arbitrary supernatural force. They reveal what divine power is for. God’s power restores communion, repairs what has been fractured, and awakens life where death has taken hold.

Christ is therefore the source of every genuinely Christian “work of wonder.” The wonder may be a physical healing, but it may also be the softening of a hardened heart, the reconciliation of enemies, the recovery of hope, or the courage to love amid suffering. The deepest miracle is the renewal of creation through love.

### The Completeness of Divine Power

To say that God’s power is complete is to say that nothing outside God is required to make God fully God. Divine life is not deficient, threatened, or in competition with creation. God creates not from need but from abundance.

This understanding distinguishes divine power from the anxious power human beings often seek. Human domination usually arises from insecurity. We try to control others because we fear losing status, safety, certainty, or identity.

God’s power does not need to prove itself. It can give itself away without being diminished. It can create genuine freedom in others without fearing their difference.

The completeness of divine power is therefore inseparable from generosity. Because God lacks nothing, God can pour out life freely. Creation is not the product of divine loneliness but the expression of inexhaustible love.

### Power Made Perfect in Weakness

The Christian understanding of complete power reaches its most paradoxical expression in the cross. Jesus does not conquer his enemies through superior violence. He refuses retaliation, bears the consequences of human cruelty, and answers hatred with forgiveness.

Paul later writes that divine power is made perfect in weakness. This does not mean that suffering is inherently good or that weakness should be romanticized. It means that God’s saving power does not depend upon the instruments by which earthly powers preserve themselves.

The cross reveals a power capable of entering violence without reproducing it. Christ absorbs hostility and responds with mercy, exposing the emptiness of domination. Resurrection then declares that self-giving love, not death, possesses the final word.

From a Christian perspective, power is complete when it is entirely free to love. A power compelled to defend itself at every threat is not yet whole. Divine power can descend, serve, forgive, and suffer because nothing can separate God from the fullness of divine life.

### Christ as the Completion of Humanity

The word *telesma*, with its connection to completion or fulfillment, also resonates with the Christian vision of Christ as the fully realized human being. Jesus does not appear as humanity diminished by divinity. He reveals what humanity looks like when wholly open to God.

His freedom is not the ability to satisfy every impulse but the capacity to love without inner division. His will, desire, body, prayer, and action move together in communion with the Father. He is powerful because he is whole.

Christian transformation is therefore not merely the attempt to obey a distant example. It is participation in Christ’s own life through the Spirit. The fragmented human person is gradually gathered into a more unified pattern of love.

This is the work of sanctification. Our thoughts, desires, habits, relationships, and bodies are brought into communion rather than left to pull in opposing directions. We do not become less ourselves; we become ourselves in greater truth.

### The Works Greater Than These

In the Gospel of John, Jesus tells his disciples that those who trust him will do the works he does and even “greater works.” This statement has sometimes been interpreted as a promise that believers will perform increasingly spectacular miracles. Yet the wider Gospel directs attention toward the spread of Christ’s reconciling life through a community formed by the Spirit.

The Church’s works of wonder are not limited to supernatural signs. A community in which social enemies share one table is itself a wonder. A person who forgives after deep injury, a wealthy person who releases possessions for the sake of the poor, or a frightened person who chooses costly courage reveals the transforming power of grace.

These wonders are often quiet and easily overlooked. They do not always draw crowds or confirm anyone’s spiritual importance. Yet they manifest a power the world cannot manufacture through coercion.

Christian power bears fruit as love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. These may sound ordinary beside tales of alchemical stones and magical talismans, but they represent the reconstruction of human life from within.

### Grace Perfects Nature

The alchemical notion that matter contains a potential waiting to be brought to completion has a partial parallel in the Christian principle that grace perfects nature. Grace does not treat creation as worthless material to be discarded. It heals, elevates, and fulfills the life God has already given.

Human beings are created for communion with God, one another, and the world. Sin distorts this vocation but does not erase it. Beneath our fear, rivalry, and self-deception remains the divine intention calling us toward wholeness.

Grace works with that created longing. It does not impose an alien identity upon us but frees us to become what love intended. The person transformed in Christ is not replaced by someone else; the true person emerges from beneath the accumulated layers of bondage.

This does not mean that human potential naturally unfolds without divine rescue or difficult conversion. Alchemical optimism can sometimes underestimate the depth of moral disorder. Christianity insists that healing comes as gift and often passes through repentance, surrender, and death to the false self.

### The Holy Spirit and the Completion of the Work

Christian transformation is not completed through willpower alone. The Holy Spirit carries the life of Christ into the depths of the human person, gradually reshaping desire and making possible what effort by itself cannot accomplish. The Spirit is both the giver of gifts and the patient artisan of character.

This work is often slower than we would prefer. We may experience moments of insight, healing, or spiritual intensity, yet discover that old fears and habits remain. Grace does not always bypass the ordinary processes through which a life is rebuilt.

The Spirit works through prayer, community, forgiveness, suffering, sacrament, Scripture, friendship, and repeated choices of love. What begins as gift becomes embodied through practice. Divine power does not eliminate human participation but awakens and sustains it.

The completeness belongs to God even while the work within us remains unfinished. We are not required to pretend that we have arrived. Our hope rests in the faithfulness of the One who continues the work.

### Wholeness as Spiritual Power

The saying connects power with completeness, and this connection offers an important insight for Christian spirituality. A divided person may possess talent, charisma, knowledge, or authority while lacking spiritual power in the deepest sense. Outer effectiveness can coexist with profound inner fragmentation.

Christian wholeness does not mean psychological perfection or freedom from every conflict. It means that the whole person is increasingly brought into the light of God. Nothing must remain permanently hidden, defended, or excluded from grace.

As this integration deepens, the person becomes freer to love. Energy once spent maintaining appearances can become compassion. Strength once used to suppress pain can become courage, while intelligence once used for self-protection can become wisdom in service of others.

The result may not appear dramatic. The truly whole person often becomes gentler rather than more imposing. Spiritual power is revealed through presence, patience, and the capacity to remain loving when fear would normally take control.

### The Church and the Temptation of Power

The language of miraculous works has often tempted religious communities to equate divine favor with visible success. Large crowds, wealth, political access, charismatic personalities, and extraordinary claims may be treated as evidence of spiritual power. Yet none of these necessarily reveals the completeness of Christ.

The New Testament repeatedly directs attention toward a different measure. Power is authenticated by love, service, truthfulness, and care for those without status. The greatest becomes the servant, and the leader takes the lowest place.

When the Church seeks influence chiefly to protect itself, defeat opponents, or impose conformity, it abandons the pattern of Christ. It may still speak of divine power while operating from fear and incompleteness.

A Christian work of wonder is not the triumph of the institution. It is the manifestation of Christ’s life. Wherever the wounded are restored, the excluded welcomed, the hungry fed, and enemies reconciled, the complete power of love becomes visible.

### A Caution About Spiritual Achievement

The saying’s language of completion can also feed spiritual perfectionism. Seekers may imagine that enlightenment, sanctity, or mystical union will eventually remove every weakness and uncertainty. The spiritual path becomes a project of constructing an invulnerable self.

Christian maturity does not produce invulnerability. Even the saints remain dependent creatures who experience grief, limitation, temptation, and need. Their holiness lies not in escaping humanity but in consenting more fully to grace within it.

The desire to become complete can itself become a refusal of love if it makes us impatient with our unfinishedness or contemptuous of others. We may try to perfect ourselves in order to become worthy of belonging, forgetting that transformation begins with being loved before the work is complete.

Christian hope rests not in our achievement but in God’s faithfulness. We participate earnestly in the work, yet we do so as people already held within mercy. Grace does not wait at the end as a prize; it accompanies every stage of becoming.

### The Ordinary Works of Wonder

The phrase “works of wonder” may call to mind visions, healings, supernatural knowledge, or dramatic transformations. Yet many of the greatest wonders occur within ordinary life. A person interrupts a generational cycle of cruelty, chooses honesty where deception would be easier, or remains tender after suffering.

Such acts may never be recognized publicly. They possess no obvious glamour and cannot be marketed as spiritual achievement. Yet they reveal a power capable of creating a future different from the past.

A family learns to speak truth without abandoning one another. A community refuses to scapegoat an outsider. A grieving person allows sorrow to deepen compassion rather than harden into bitterness.

These are works of completion because they restore relationships to their intended form. They bring fragments into communion. The power accomplishing them is complete because its source is the inexhaustible love of God.

### Contemplative Synthesis

The sixth saying declares that the mysterious One Thing is the source of every perfected or wondrous work and that its power is whole. The alchemist saw here the philosopher’s stone, a substance brought to such harmony that it could communicate perfection to other matter. The magician might see the source of talismanic effectiveness, while the psychologist may recognize the power released through inner integration.

Christian contemplation receives the longing behind these interpretations but relocates complete power in the life of God revealed through Christ. Divine power is creative, healing, reconciling, and self-giving. It does not seek domination because it lacks nothing and fears nothing.

The greatest wonder is therefore not mastery over nature or escape from human limitation. It is the birth of love within places ruled by fear. It is the gathering of fragmented lives into communion and the renewal of creation from within.

Christ reveals that complete power and complete love are one. The power of God becomes visible in healing hands, a servant’s towel, a forgiving voice, a broken body, and an empty tomb. It descends into weakness without being defeated and enters death without surrendering life.

The saying invites us to reconsider what we mean by spiritual power. If our power requires others to become smaller, it is not whole. If our wonder serves our ego, it has not reached completion.

The power of Christ is complete because it can give itself away entirely and remain inexhaustibly love.

### Questions for Reflection

What kind of power am I most tempted to seek?

Do I associate spiritual maturity with control, certainty, or invulnerability?

Where is inner fragmentation consuming energy that could be given to love?

What quiet work of wonder may God already be accomplishing in my life?

How might grace be drawing an unfinished part of me toward wholeness?

Do the works I admire resemble the self-giving life of Christ?

### Prayer

God of inexhaustible life,
You are the source
of every work of healing,
beauty, mercy, and renewal.

Gather what is divided within me.
Free me from the desire
to control, impress, or dominate.
Teach me the power
that gives itself away in love.

Form the life of Christ within me
until fear becomes courage,
resentment becomes mercy,
and separation becomes communion.

Holy Spirit, complete Your work
through the ordinary faithfulness
of my embodied life.

May every gift I receive
become a gift for others,
and may every work of wonder
bear the humble likeness of Christ.

Amen.
